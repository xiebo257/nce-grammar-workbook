Unit 49 — Do you know where...? I don't know what... etc.

What it means

Indirect (embedded) questions are questions inside other sentences — after phrases like "Do you know...", "I don't know...", "Can you tell me...". They use statement word order (subject + verb), not question word order (verb + subject).

TypeFormExample
Wh- indirect questionDo you know + where/what/why + subject + verbDo you know where she is?
if indirect questionDo you know + if + subject + verbDo you know if they are coming?
NegativeI don't know + where/what + subject + verbI don't know where Susan works.
When to use indirect questions
  • After Do you know...? / I don't know... / Can you tell me...?
  • When you want to ask something less directly / more politely
  • When reporting what someone else might know
Why no inversion in indirect questions

In a direct question, the verb inverts with the subject: "Where is she?" / "Where does Susan work?" But in an indirect question, the main question is already carried by "Do you know?" — so the embedded part uses normal statement order:

Direct questionIndirect question
Where is she?Do you know where she is?
Where does Susan work?I don't know where Susan works.
What time does the meeting begin?Do you know what time the meeting begins?

No auxiliary "do/does/did" in the indirect question — the main verb carries the tense and person.

Pitfalls
  • Keep the same tense as the direct question: "Has Emily lived here?" → "I don't know how long she has lived here." (not "lived")
  • 3rd person singular still takes -s: "where Susan works" (not "work"), "he gets up" (not "get")
  • Use if for yes/no questions: "Do you know if they went to Jane's party?"
  • No "does/do/did" inside the indirect question — the verb itself shows tense and person
Correct examples
  • Do you know what time the meeting begins? (not "does the meeting begin")
  • I don't know where Susan works. (not "where Susan work")
  • Do you know if they went to Jane's party? (not "if they went go")
  • I don't know how long she has lived here. (keep present perfect)
Form rules
  • Wh- word + subject + verb: where she is / what time he gets up / how long she has lived
  • if + subject + verb (for yes/no): if they went / if she is coming
  • No auxiliary do/does/did inside the indirect question
  • Keep the same tense as the original direct question
  • 3rd person singular: verb still takes -s (Susan works, the meeting begins)

Unit 50 — She said that... He told me that...

What it means

Reported speech (indirect speech) reports what someone said, without quoting them directly. Tenses shift back one step and pronouns change to match the perspective of the reporter.

Direct speechReported speechChange
"I can't go."She said she couldn't go.can → could
"I don't feel well."She said she didn't feel well.present → past
"I will call."He said he would call.will → would
"I have finished."He said he had finished.present perfect → past perfect
When to use reported speech
  • When reporting what someone said in the past: "She said that she couldn't go."
  • "that" is optional: "She said she couldn't go." = "She said that she couldn't go."
  • Pronouns shift to match the reporter's perspective: "I" → "she/he", "my" → "her/his"
Why tenses shift back one step

Because the reporting verb (said) is in the past, the speech being reported happened before now. To show this time gap, tenses shift back one step:

DirectReportedRule
present simplepast simplework → worked
present perfectpast perfecthave worked → had worked
willwouldwill go → would go
cancouldcan go → could go

Past doesn't change: "I went" → "She said she went." (past simple stays past simple).

say vs tell — the object rule

VerbPatternExample
sayno object (or "that")She said (that) she couldn't go.
tellwith objectShe told me that she couldn't go.
Pitfalls
  • Typo: sad ≠ said — "sad" = emotion (adjective), "said" = spoke (verb)
  • Past simple doesn't change: "I went" → "She said she went" (stays "went")
  • "said" stays "said" (past doesn't shift further back)
  • Don't forget the period at the end of the sentence
Form rules
  • say (no object): She said that... / She said she...
  • tell (with object): She told me that... / He told them to...
  • Tense shift: present → past, can → could, will → would, PP → past perfect
  • Past simple stays: "I went" → "she went"
  • Pronouns: I → he/she, my → his/her, we → they

Unit 51 — work/working go/going do/doing

What it means

Different verbs and triggers require different verb forms after them: bare infinitive, to + infinitive, or -ing.

TriggerFormExamples
modals (can, must, should, might)bare infinitiveI can swim. / She must go. / You should rest.
want / would like / have to / used toto + infinitiveI want to go. / Would you like to come?
be (am/is/are/was/were)-ing (continuous)I am working. / She was reading.
enjoy / finish / stop / mind-ing (gerund)I enjoy reading. / Stop talking.
When to use which form
  • After modals (can, could, must, should, might) → bare infinitive: can swim, must go
  • After want, would like, have to, used to, need → to + infinitive: want to go, would like to come
  • After be (am, is, are, was, were) → -ing (continuous): am working, was reading
  • After enjoy, finish, stop, mind, suggest → -ing (gerund): enjoy reading, finish eating
Why would like always takes "to + infinitive"

"Would like" is a polite way to say "want". Both "want" and "would like" are followed by to + infinitive, never a bare infinitive:

  • I want to go. (not "I want go")
  • Would you like to come? (not "Would you like come?")

The "to" is part of the infinitive form — it doesn't mean "towards". It's grammatically required.

Pitfalls
  • watch TV (visual) vs listen to radio (audio) — match verb to media type
  • "would like" → always to + infinitive (never bare): "Would you like to go?" not "Would you like go?"
  • Spelling: Paul (not Pual) — common transposition error
Correct examples
  • I'm not listening to the radio. (audio → listen, not watch)
  • Would you like to go on a trip? (would like + to + inf)
  • I want to phone Paul. (want + to + inf)
  • I can swim. (modal + bare inf)
Form rules
  • Modal + bare infinitive: can work / must go / should rest
  • want/would like/have to/used to + to + infinitive: want to work / would like to go
  • be + -ing: am working / was going
  • enjoy/finish/stop/mind + -ing: enjoy working / stop talking

Unit 52 — to... (I want to do) and -ing (I enjoy doing)

What it means

Some verbs are followed by to + infinitive, others by -ing (gerund). There's no logical rule — you have to learn which verbs take which pattern.

Verb + to + infinitiveVerb + -ing (gerund)Both
want to goenjoy readinglike to read / like reading
hope to seefinish workinglove to swim / love swimming
decide to leavestop talkinghate to wait / hate waiting
promise to helpmind openingstart to rain / start raining
refuse / learn / try / forget / expect / offersuggest / keep / consider / practiseprefer / begin / continue
When to use to + inf vs -ing
  • Check the main verb before the blank
  • If it's "want, hope, decide, promise, refuse, learn, try, forget, expect, offer" → to + infinitive
  • If it's "enjoy, finish, stop, mind, suggest, keep, consider, practise" → -ing
  • If it's "like, love, hate, start, prefer, begin, continue" → both work
Why "go shopping" has no "to"

"Shopping" is an activity (gerund), not a place. When we use "go" with activities, the pattern is go + -ing with no "to":

  • go shopping (not "go to shopping")
  • go swimming (not "go to swimming")
  • go skiing / go fishing / go running

But if you mention a place, use "to": "go to the shops" / "go to the pool".

Pitfalls
  • "go to shopping" → wrong! "go shopping" (activity = gerund, no "to")
  • "enjoy to read" → wrong! "enjoy reading" (enjoy always takes -ing)
  • "go to the shops" (place) vs "go shopping" (activity) — choose based on what follows
  • "I'd love to go there" preferred over "I'd love to be there" (movement matches "been to")
Correct examples
  • I'd like to go shopping. (go + -ing, no "to" before activity)
  • I enjoy reading. (enjoy + -ing)
  • I want to leave. (want + to + inf)
  • I'd love to go there one day. (movement: go, not be)
Form rules
  • to + infinitive: want, hope, decide, promise, refuse, learn, try, forget, expect, offer
  • -ing: enjoy, finish, stop, mind, suggest, keep, consider, practise
  • both: like, love, hate, start, prefer, begin, continue
  • go + -ing for activities: go shopping, go swimming, go skiing (no "to")

Unit 53 — I want you to... I told you to...

What it means

Some verbs use the pattern verb + object + to + infinitive — you want/tell someone to do something.

VerbPatternExample
wantwant + object + to + infI want you to help me.
telltell + object + to + infI told him to go.
adviseadvise + object + to + infI advised him to see a doctor.
askask + object + to + infI asked her to wait.
teachteach + object + to + infShe taught me to drive.
persuadepersuade + object + to + infI persuaded him to stay.
When to use verb + object + to + infinitive
  • When telling/asking/wanting someone to do something
  • The object receives the request/command: "I want him to go" (he receives the wish)
  • Use object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them) — not subject pronouns (I, he, she, we, they)
Why object pronouns after verbs like advised/told/asked

These verbs act on someone — the person is the object of the verb, so you need an object pronoun:

Subject pronounObject pronounExample
ImeShe told me to go.
hehimI advised him to rest.
sheherI asked her to wait.
weusThey told us to leave.
theythemWe wanted them to help.

let and make — the exceptions

VerbPatternExample
letlet + object + bare infinitive (no "to")She let me go. (not "to go")
makemake + object + bare infinitive (no "to")He made me laugh. (not "to laugh")
Pitfalls
  • he → him (object pronoun after advised/told/asked): "I advised him" not "I advised he"
  • let/make take bare infinitive (no "to"): "She let me go" not "She let me to go"
  • Instruments always take "the": play the piano / the guitar / the violin
  • Reported speech must match what was actually said: "Come back" → "I told him to come back" (not "to wait for me")
Correct examples
  • I advised him to go to the doctor. (object pronoun: him, not he)
  • I told him to come back in ten minutes. (matches what was said)
  • Amy's mother taught her to play the piano. (instrument takes "the")
  • She let me drive her car. (let + bare infinitive)
Form rules
  • want/tell/advise/ask/teach/persuade + object pronoun + to + infinitive
  • Object pronouns: me, him, her, us, them (never he/she/they after these verbs)
  • let/make + object + bare infinitive (no "to")
  • Instruments: play the piano / the guitar / the violin

Unit 54 — I went to the shop to...

What it means

Use to + infinitive to express purpose — why you did something. "I went to the shop to buy milk." = I went there because I wanted to buy milk.

PatternUseExample
to + infinitivepurpose with a verbI went to the shop to buy milk.
for + nounpurpose with a nounI went to the shop for milk.
for + object + to + infinitivepurpose with a personI'm waiting for the film to begin.
When to use to vs for
What follows?UseExample
a verbto + infinitiveI went out to buy a newspaper.
a nounfor + nounI went out for a newspaper.
an object + actionfor + object + to + infI'm waiting for him to arrive.
Why "to" before verbs and "for" before nouns

"to" is the infinitive marker (it goes with verbs). "for" is a preposition (it goes with nouns).

  • I went to London to see my friend. (verb → to)
  • I went to London for a holiday. (noun → for)
  • I'm waiting for the bus to come. (object + verb → for + object + to + inf)
Pitfalls
  • "home" is an adverb — no "to" before it: "go home" (not "go to home"). "I went home to rest."
  • Indirect questions in purpose clauses: "to see who it was" (subject + verb, past tense) — not "who is it" (inverted, present)
  • "go shopping" (no "to" before activity — see Unit 52)
  • Spelling: friend (not freind) — "i before e except after c"
Correct examples
  • I went to a coffee shop to meet a friend.
  • The doorbell rang, I looked out the window to see who it was. (indirect question: subject + verb, past tense)
  • Do I need a key to open this door?
  • I went home to rest. (no "to" before home)
Form rules
  • to + infinitive (purpose with verb): to buy, to see, to open
  • for + noun (purpose with noun): for milk, for a holiday
  • for + object + to + infinitive: for the film to begin, for him to arrive
  • "home" = adverb (no "to"): go home, come home, went home
  • Indirect questions in purpose clauses: subject + verb, correct tense ("who it was", not "who is it")
