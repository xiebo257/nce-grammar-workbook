Unit 25 — Present continuous for future

What it means

The present continuous (be + -ing) is used for fixed future arrangements — things you have already decided and arranged with someone.

FormUseExample
am/is/are + -ingfixed future arrangementI'm playing tennis tomorrow.
present simpletimetables / schedulesThe train leaves at 8.
When to use present continuous for future
  • The arrangement is already made with someone (a person, a venue, a group) → present continuous
  • "I'm meeting Sue tomorrow." = we agreed to meet
  • "I'm going to the cinema tonight." = the plan exists
  • "The course finishes on Friday." = timetable → present simple
Why present continuous for future — the plan pre-exists

When you use present continuous for the future, it means the decision was already made before the conversation. The arrangement already exists — you're just reporting it. This is different from will (decision made now) or going to (intention formed earlier).

Pitfall — no preposition before "tomorrow + time of day"

❌ "I'm going to the cinema at tomorrow night." — wrong!

✅ "I'm going to the cinema tomorrow night." — correct!

"Tomorrow night", "tomorrow morning", "tomorrow afternoon" are adverbial phrases — they don't need a preposition. Drop "at", "in", and "on" before these expressions.

Pitfall — 3rd person singular in present simple timetables

❌ "The course finish on Friday." — missing -es!

✅ "The course finishes on Friday." — the course = it → add -es.

❌ "The last bus leave at midnight." — missing -s!

✅ "The last bus leaves at midnight." — the bus = it → add -s.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "I'm having lunch with Will." (present continuous for arrangement)
  • ✅ "Sue and Tom are going to a party." (plural subject → "are")
  • ✅ "I'm staying at home on Saturday." (correct spelling)
Form rules
  • am/is/are + verb-ing: I'm playing / she's having / they are going
  • Plural subjects (Sue and Tom, we, they) → are
  • Present simple for timetables: he/she/it → add -s/-es (finishes, leaves)
  • No preposition before "tomorrow + time of day": tomorrow night, tomorrow afternoon

Unit 26 — I'm going to...

What it means

am/is/are going to + infinitive expresses intentions (things you've decided to do) and predictions based on visible evidence.

PatternUseExample
going to + verb (intention)decision already madeI'm going to buy a new car. (I decided last week)
going to + verb (prediction)prediction from visible evidenceLook at that shelf — it's going to fall!
am/is/are going toformI am / He is / They are going to ...
When to use going to vs will vs present continuous
SituationFormExample
Decision made before nowgoing toI'm going to buy a car. (told you last week)
Decision made right nowwillI'll carry it for you. (just decided)
Arrangement made with someonepresent continuousI'm meeting Sue at 8. (agreed)
Prediction from visible evidencegoing toThe shelf is going to fall. (I can see it)
Why "going to" = the decision pre-exists

"Going to" means the decision was made before this conversation. "Carl is going to buy a new car. He told me last week." The fact that he told you last week signals this is a pre-existing intention. If he decided now, you'd use "will".

Pitfall — bath vs batch

❌ "I am going to have a batch." — wrong! A "batch" is a group of things.

✅ "I am going to have a bath." — correct! A "bath" is washing yourself.

WordMeaningExample
bathwashing yourselfhave a bath / take a bath
batcha group processed togethera batch of cookies / a batch of files
Correct examples
  • ✅ "I'm going to have a bath." (not "batch")
  • ✅ "Look at those clouds! It's going to rain." (visible evidence)
  • ✅ "I'm going to study tonight." (pre-decided intention)
Form rules
  • am/is/are + going to + bare infinitive: going to buy / going to fall / going to have
  • Plural subjects → "are going to" (Sue and Tom are going to...)
  • Negative: am not / isn't / aren't going to

Unit 27 — will/shall 1

What it means

will + infinitive expresses future facts and predictions. 'll = will, won't = will not.

PatternUseExample
will + verbfuture factShe will be 25 next week.
I think ... willpredictionI think it will rain tomorrow.
'll (contraction)short form of willI'll see you tomorrow.
won'tnegative (will not)He won't come.
When to use will vs going to vs present continuous
SituationFormExample
Future fact (certain)willShe'll be 25 next week.
Prediction (opinion, no visible evidence)willI think it will rain.
Prediction from visible evidencegoing toLook — it's going to rain! (dark clouds)
Arrangement (made with someone)present continuousI'm meeting Sue at 8.
Why will for facts and predictions, not for arrangements

will = a future fact or a prediction based on opinion (I think it will rain). It does not mean you have arranged anything. For arrangements already made with a person, use the present continuous (I'm meeting Sue). For decisions already made (intentions), use going to.

Pitfall — always include the subject

❌ Writing only "'ll be" — missing subject!

✅ "She'll be" — always include the subject.

In English, the subject is mandatory. Even when the exercise provides context ("complete with she was, she's or she'll be"), you must write the full form including "she" — not just the verb part.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "She'll be here in an hour." (future fact + subject)
  • ✅ "She was a teacher." (past + subject)
  • ✅ "She's at home." (present + subject)
  • ✅ "I think she'll win." (prediction + subject)
Form rules
  • will + bare infinitive: will go / will be / will rain
  • Contractions: 'll = will (she'll, I'll, they'll)
  • Negative: won't = will not (no "willn't")
  • Question: Will you...? / Will she...?
  • Always include the subject: she'll be, not just "'ll be"

Unit 28 — will/shall 2

What it means

will is used for offers, instant decisions, and suggestions. Shall I...? = offer; Shall we...? = suggestion.

PatternUseExample
I'll + verbinstant decision (made now)"Which one?" — "I'll take this one."
I'll + verboffer to helpI'll carry it for you.
I think I'llpositive decisionI think I'll buy it.
I don't think I'llnegative decisionI don't think I'll buy it.
Shall I...?offer / asking permissionShall I open the window?
Shall we...?suggestionShall we go for a walk?
When to use will vs going to vs present continuous
SituationFormWhy
Instant decision (just now)willborn in the conversation
Pre-decided intentiongoing todecision made before
Fixed arrangement (with person)present continuousplan already agreed
  • "I haven't done the shopping." → "I'll do it later." = will (just decided)
  • "Carl is going to buy a car. He told me last week." = going to (pre-decided)
  • "I like sport. I watch a lot of sport on TV." = present simple (general fact, not future)
Why will = "born in the conversation"

When you use will, the decision is created at the moment of speaking. "I haven't done the shopping." → "I'll do it later." The decision to do it later is made now, in response to the statement. Compare: "Carl is going to buy a car. He told me last week." — the decision was made before the conversation.

Pitfall — buy vs but

❌ "I don't think I'll but." — wrong word! "but" is a conjunction.

✅ "I don't think I'll buy." — correct! "buy" is a verb (to purchase).

These sound similar but have completely different meanings. Always check if the word is a verb (buy) or conjunction (but).

Pitfall — write the full option in "which is right" exercises

When choosing between two options, write the complete answer, not just the key word:

You wroteFull optionIssue
I'llI'll doMissing "do"
watchI watchMissing "I"
to buyis going to buyMissing "is going"
Correct examples
  • ✅ "I don't think I'll buy it." (verb, not "but")
  • ✅ "I haven't done the shopping." → "I'll do it later." (full option)
  • ✅ "Shall I open the window?" (offer)
  • ✅ "Shall we go for a walk?" (suggestion)
Form rules
  • will + bare infinitive: I'll carry / I'll buy / Shall I open?
  • I think I'll + verb (positive decision)
  • I don't think I'll + verb (negative decision — "not" after "think", not after "will")
  • Shall I...? for offers / Shall we...? for suggestions

Unit 29 — might

What it means

might + bare infinitive expresses uncertain possibility — "maybe, but I'm not sure." It means something is possible but not certain (roughly 30-40% certainty).

PatternUseExample
might + verbuncertain possibilityI might go to the cinema. (maybe)
might not + verbuncertain negativeMark might not be here. (maybe he won't)
I'm not sure ... mighthedging answer"When will you see Kate?" — "I'm not sure. I might see her on Monday."
When to use might
  • When you're not sure → might: "I might go to the cinema tomorrow."
  • When you haven't decided → might: "I haven't decided yet. I might cook."
  • When something is possible but not certain → might: "It might rain later."
  • Certainty scale: will (100%) → might (30-40%) → won't (0%)
Why might = "maybe (30-40%)"

might sits between certainty and impossibility. "I'll go" = I'm going (certain). "I might go" = maybe I will, maybe I won't (uncertain). "I won't go" = I'm not going (certain not to). Use might when you genuinely don't know — the typical clues are "I'm not sure" or "I haven't decided".

Pitfall — might + bare infinitive (never -ing)

❌ "I might cooking." — wrong! Modal + -ing is never correct.

✅ "I might cook." — correct! Modal + bare infinitive.

might is a modal verb — like can, could, should, will. All modals take the bare infinitive (no "to", no -ing, no -s). Always "might + verb": might cook / might go / might be.

Pitfall — match pronouns to names

❌ "I might see him on Monday." (about Kate) — wrong! Kate is female → her.

❌ "I might not be here." (about Mark) — wrong subject! The sentence is about Mark, not "I".

Always check who the sentence is about and choose the correct pronoun:

NamePronoun
Kateshe / her
Markhe / him
Sue and Tomthey / them
Pitfall — spelling: cinema

❌ "cenima" → ✅ "cinema" — correct spelling: c-i-n-e-m-a.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "Mark might not be here next week." (subject = Mark, not "I")
  • ✅ "I might see her on Monday." (Kate → her, not him)
  • ✅ "I might cook." (bare infinitive, not -ing)
  • ✅ "I might go to the cinema." (correct spelling)
Form rules
  • might + bare infinitive: might cook / might go / might be (never -ing, never "to")
  • Negative: might not (no contraction — "mightn't" is rare/archaic)
  • No -s for 3rd person: he might go (not "he mights go")
  • Question: Do you think ... might...? (not "Might you...?" in modern English)
