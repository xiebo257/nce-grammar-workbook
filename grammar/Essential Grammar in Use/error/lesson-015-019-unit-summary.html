Unit 15 — I have done (present perfect 1)

What it means

The present perfect connects a past action to the present: have/has + past participle. The action happened at some point before now, and the result matters now.

SubjectAuxiliaryFormExample
I / you / we / theyhavehave + past participleI have finished my homework.
he / she / ithashas + past participleShe has gone out.
questionHave / Hasaux + subject + participleHave you seen Paula?
negativehaven't / hasn'taux + not + participleI haven't told anyone.
When to use the present perfect
  • The action happened (we don't say exactly when)
  • The result matters now: "She's not here. She has gone out." (result: she is absent now)
  • You don't specify a past time (no "yesterday", "last week" — that would be past simple)
Why present perfect, not past simple

The present perfect focuses on the result, not on when it happened. Past simple focuses on a specific finished time:

TenseFocusExample
present perfectresult now"Somebody has taken my umbrella." (it's gone now)
past simplewhen it happened"Somebody took my umbrella yesterday." (specific time)

If you can add "yesterday" → past simple. If the result is what matters → present perfect.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "She's not here. She has gone out." (result: absent now)
  • ✅ "Have you seen Paula?" (question: result — do you know where she is?)
  • ✅ "I have finished with this magazine. Do you want it?" (result: it's available now)
Pitfalls
  • ❌ Using a verb not in the box: "Somebody has stolen it" — wrong because "steal" is not given; use "take" → "has taken it".
  • ❌ Wrong verb for context: "I have taken with this magazine. Do you want it?" — "take" doesn't fit. The context "Do you want it?" → the speaker is finished with it.
  • ❌ Capitalization: "have you seen her?" → "Have you seen her?" (capitalize at sentence/question start).
Form rules
  • have/has + past participle: have gone / has taken / have finished
  • Regular verbs: -ed (finish → finished, decide → decided)
  • Irregular verbs: memorize the three forms (see → saw → seen, write → wrote → written, take → took → taken)
  • Question: Have/Has + subject + participle — always capitalize at sentence start
  • Short answer: "Yes, I have." / "No, she hasn't." (repeat auxiliary, not the main verb)

Unit 16 — I've just/already/yet (present perfect 2)

What it means

Three adverbs scope the present perfect to specific time frames:

AdverbMeaningPositionExample
justmoments ago (very recent)mid position (after aux)She has just left.
alreadysooner than expectedmid position (after aux)I've already seen it.
yetup to now (expecting it)end positionHave you done it yet? / I haven't done it yet.
When to use each adverb
  • just → positive statements: the action happened very recently. "I've just finished."
  • already → positive statements: it happened sooner than expected. "Don't tell me — I already know."
  • yet → negative and question only: "I haven't eaten yet." / "Have you eaten yet?"

Position: just and already go after have/has, before the participle. yet goes at the end.

Why these adverbs scope the present perfect

These adverbs narrow the time frame of the present perfect:

AdverbScopeWhy present perfect
justthe last few minutesresult is fresh — relevant right now
alreadybefore expected timesurprise — it's done already
yetup to this momentstill expecting it to happen

All three connect a past action to now — that's why present perfect is the right tense.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "Has Tom sold his car yet?" (question, proper noun capitalized, yet at end)
  • ✅ "I've already told him." (positive, already after auxiliary)
  • ✅ "She's just gone out." (positive, just after auxiliary)
Pitfalls
  • ❌ "Has tom sold his car yet?" → ✅ "Has Tom..." — proper nouns must be capitalized.
  • ❌ "yet" in positive statements → yet only works in negatives and questions.
  • ❌ "already" in questions (usually) → use already in positive statements; use yet in questions.
Form rules
  • just/already: have/has + just/already + past participle (mid position)
  • yet: have/has + subject + not + participle + yet (end position, negatives/questions only)
  • Contractions: I've = I have, she's = she has, he's = he has, they've = they have

Unit 17 — Have you ever...? (present perfect 3)

What it means

The present perfect is used for life experience — things that have happened (or never happened) at any time up to now.

PatternUseExample
Have you ever...?"at any time in your life"Have you ever been to Japan?
I have never..."not at any time"I've never eaten sushi.
She has...life experience (positive)She has traveled all over the world.
When to use present perfect for experience
  • No specific past time is mentioned — the experience spans your whole life up to now.
  • ever = "at any time" (used in questions)
  • never = "not at any time" (used in positive statements with negative meaning)
  • "She has had an interesting life." → present perfect (life experience up to now)
Why present perfect, not past simple, for "ever/never"

"Have you ever been to Japan?" — you're asking about any time in the person's life, which includes the present moment (their life is still going). Past simple would require a specific time: "Did you go to Japan last year?" Present perfect with ever/never covers the entire span up to now.

QuestionScopeTense
"Have you ever been to Japan?"any time in your lifepresent perfect
"Did you go to Japan last year?"specific past timepast simple

gone vs been — the #1 confusion

FormMeaningWhere is the person now?Example
has gonewent and is still therenot here"She's gone to Japan." (she's still there)
has beenwent and came back (experience)here"She's been to Japan many times." (visited and returned)
Correct examples
  • ✅ "She has traveled all over the world." (regular past participle: travel → traveled)
  • ✅ "She has written ten books." (irregular: write → wrote → written)
  • ✅ "Rebecca loves Paris. She's been there many times." (repeated visits = been, not gone)
Pitfalls
  • ❌ "She has wrote ten books." → ✅ "She has written ten books." — irregular past participle: write → wrote → written. "has wrote" is never correct.
  • ❌ "She's gone there many times." → ✅ "She's been there many times." — "many times" means repeated visits (gone = still there, which contradicts "many times").
  • ❌ "has traveld" → ✅ "has traveled" — spelling: double the consonant before -ed (travel → traveled/travelled).
Form rules
  • have/has + past participle (same as other present perfect units)
  • ever/never go in mid position (after auxiliary, before participle): "Have you ever eaten...?"
  • Irregular verbs — memorize all three forms:
    BasePast simplePast participle
    writewrotewritten
    gowentgone / been
    traveltraveledtraveled
    meetmetmet
    havehadhad

Unit 18 — How long have you...? (present perfect 4)

What it means

The present perfect with for/since describes states that started in the past and continue up to now.

PatternUseExample
How long have you...?asking about duration of a stateHow long have you been a teacher?
for + durationlength of timeI've lived here for three years.
since + start pointwhen it beganI've lived here since 2020.
When to use present perfect for duration
  • State verbs (be, know, live, work, have) use present perfect simple for duration: "I've known her for ten years."
  • for + length of time (for three days, for a long time)
  • since + starting point (since Monday, since 2018, since I was a child)
  • The state started in the past and continues now: "She has been in Canada for two years." (and she's still there)
Why pronoun substitution matters in questions

When you make a question from someone's statement, the subject changes:

StatementQuestionShift
"I am a teacher.""How long have you been a teacher?"I → you
"Helen lives in London.""How long has she lived in London?"Helen → she
"Steve and Nadia know each other.""How long have they known each other?"Steve and Nadia → they
"My brother lives in Canada.""How long has he been in Canada?"My brother → he

Rule: The book uses pronouns (he, she, they, you) in the answer key, not full nouns ("your brother"). Match the expected pronoun.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "How long has he been in Canada?" (not "your brother" — use pronoun)
  • ✅ "How long have you been a teacher?" (not "have I" — I → you)
  • ✅ "How long have they known each other?" (Steve and Nadia → they)
Pitfalls
  • ❌ "How long has your brother been in Canada?" → ✅ "How long has he..." — the book expects the pronoun "he" (like "she" for Helen).
  • ❌ "How long have you been a teacher." → ✅ "...a teacher?" — don't add a period when a question mark is already present in the prompt.
Form rules
  • How long + have/has + subject + past participle
  • State verbs (be, know, live, work) use present perfect simple for duration
  • for + duration (for two years, for a long time)
  • since + starting point (since 2020, since Monday, since I was young)

Unit 19 — for/since/ago

What it means

Three different ways to talk about time and duration:

WordMeaningTenseExample
forlength of time (duration)present perfect / past simpleI've lived here for three years.
sincestarting point (when it began)present perfectI've lived here since 2020.
agomeasured back from nowpast simpleI moved here three years ago.
When to use for vs since vs ago
TestUseExample
How long? (duration)forfor three days / for a week / for a long time
When did it start? (point)sincesince Monday / since 2018 / since I was seven
How long ago? (back from now)agothree weeks ago / two days ago / a year ago
  • for + duration (a length: three days, ten years)
  • since + start point (a moment: Monday, 2020, "I was seven")
  • ago + past simple (measured back from now: "three years ago")
Why for, since, and ago are different

These three words measure time in different ways:

WordMeasuresAnalogyTense
forlength of timea ruler (how long)present perfect or past simple
sincestart pointa calendar date (when it began)present perfect
agocount back from nowa countdown (how far back)past simple

"for three years" and "since 2020" can describe the same situation — for names the length, since names the start. "three years ago" uses past simple and counts backward from now.

Correct examples
  • ✅ "David has played the piano since he was 7." (active present perfect)
  • ✅ "David has been playing the piano since he was 7." (active present perfect continuous)
  • ✅ "I've had this phone for two years." (possession + for + duration)
  • ✅ "I moved here three years ago." (past simple + ago)
Pitfalls
  • ❌ "David has been played the piano since he was 7." → ✅ "David has played..." — "has been played" is passive (the piano is played by someone). For a person doing an activity, use active: has played / has been playing.
  • ❌ "I've had lunch since 12:00." → Awkward — "lunch" is an event, not a possession. Better: "I've had this phone for two years" or "I've had a headache since morning."
  • ❌ Mixing up for/since: "I've lived here since three years" → ✅ "I've lived here for three years" (duration → for).
Form rules
  • for + duration: for three days, for ten years, for a long time
  • since + start point: since Monday, since 2020, since I was a child
  • ago goes after the duration + past simple: "I arrived three days ago."
  • ago always uses past simple (not present perfect): "I moved here three years ago." (not "I have moved here three years ago")
  • for/since use present perfect for ongoing states: "I've lived here for/since..."
