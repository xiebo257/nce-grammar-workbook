Unit 44 — is it...? have you...? do they...? (questions 1)

What it means

Questions are formed by inverting the subject and auxiliary verb. The auxiliary comes first, then the subject, then the rest.

StatementQuestionAuxiliary
You are late.Are you late?be (am/is/are)
She has finished.Has she finished?have/has
They do their homework.Do they do their homework?do/does
You can swim.Can you swim?can/could/will
He was late.Was he late?was/were
When to use inversion
  • Asking a yes/no question → invert auxiliary + subject: "Are you late?" / "Do they work?"
  • With question words (where, what, when, how, why, who) → question word + auxiliary + subject: "Where are you going?" / "What do you want?"
  • No auxiliary needed when the main verb is "be": "Are you happy?" (not "Do you are happy?")
  • do/does/did is the auxiliary for present/past simple when there is no other auxiliary: "Do you live here?" / "Does she work?"
Why inversion marks a question

In English, word order changes to signal that a sentence is a question rather than a statement. The auxiliary verb moves to the front, before the subject. This inversion is the primary grammatical signal of a question in English — it tells the listener "I am asking, not stating."

Pitfalls
  • ❌ "Was you late?" → ✅ "Were you late?" — "you" always takes were (never was), even when referring to one person.
  • ❌ "Are your parents going on holiday?" → ✅ "Are they going on holiday?" — use a pronoun instead of repeating the noun.
  • Don't use "do" with "be": ❌ "Do you are happy?" → ✅ "Are you happy?"
Form rules
  • be: am/is/are + subject — "Are you...?" / "Is she...?"
  • have: have/has + subject + past participle — "Have you finished?"
  • do/does/did + subject + bare verb — "Do they work?" / "Does he live here?"
  • modals (can, could, will, would, should) + subject + bare verb — "Can you swim?"
  • Question word + auxiliary + subject — "Where do you live?" / "What is she doing?"

Unit 45 — Who saw you? Who did you see? (questions 2)

What it means

When who or what is the subject of the question, no auxiliary is needed (subject question). When who or what is the object, an auxiliary is needed (object question).

TypePatternExample
Subject questionWho/What + verbWho broke the window?
Object questionWho/What + auxiliary + subject + verbWho did you see?
Subject (thing)What + verbWhat woke you up?
Object (thing)What + auxiliary + subject + verbWhat did you buy?
When to use no auxiliary vs with auxiliary
Is who/what the...Auxiliary?Example
subject (doing the action)NO auxiliary"Who broke the window?" (who = the person who broke it)
object (receiving the action)auxiliary needed"Who did you see?" (you = subject, who = the person seen)

Test: If "who" is doing the action → no "did". If "who" is receiving the action → needs "did".

Why subject questions don't need inversion

In a subject question, the question word is already at the front of the sentence (in the subject position). The word order is already correct for a question — the "subject" (who/what) comes first, then the verb. No inversion is needed because the sentence structure already signals "who performed this action?"

  • "Who broke the window?" — who = subject, broke = verb. Already in question order.
  • "Who did you see?" — you = subject, who = object. Needs "did" to invert.
Pitfalls
  • ❌ "Who woke me up?" (for "something woke me up") → ✅ "What woke me up?" — something → what (things); somebody → who (people).
  • ❌ "Who did break the window?" → ✅ "Who broke the window?" — subject questions don't use "did".
  • ❌ "What did happen?" → ✅ "What happened?" — subject question, no "did".
Form rules
  • Subject question: Who/What + verb (+ object) — "Who saw you?" / "What happened?"
  • Object question: Who/What + do/does/did + subject + bare verb — "Who did you see?" / "What did you buy?"
  • With "be" as main verb: "Who is that?" / "What is this?"
  • With continuous/perfect: "Who is talking?" / "What has happened?"

Unit 46 — Who is she talking to? What is it like? (questions 3)

What it means

Prepositions go at the end of questions in English. Also covers "What is it like?" (asking for a description) and "Which...?" (choosing from a set).

PatternExampleMeaning
Who + auxiliary + subject + ... + preposition?Who is she talking to?Asking about the person at the other end
What + auxiliary + subject + ... + preposition?What are you looking at?Asking about the thing being looked at
What + be + subject + like?What is the weather like?Asking for a description
Which + noun?Which restaurant is he going to?Choosing from a known set
When to use prepositions at the end
  • When asking about a person with a preposition → "Who ... to/with/about/for?" — "Who did you go with?" / "Who is she talking to?"
  • When asking about a thing with a preposition → "What ... at/for/on/in?" — "What are you looking at?" / "What is it like?"
  • When asking for a description → "What is [subject] like?" — "What is the weather like?" / "What was the film like?"
Why English puts prepositions at the end

In formal/old-fashioned English, prepositions could come at the beginning: "To whom are you speaking?" In modern English, the preposition stays at the end: "Who are you speaking to?" This is because English moves the question word (who/what) to the front but leaves the preposition behind in its natural position — at the end of the clause.

Pitfalls
  • ❌ "are you going to" (picture shows a man) → ✅ "is he going to" — match the pronoun to the picture subject (man → he), not "you".
  • ❌ "Which team is he play for?" → ✅ "Which team does he play for?" — present simple question = does + base verb, not "is + base verb".
  • ❌ "waether" → ✅ "weather" — spelling.
  • "play the piano" — instruments always take "the": play the piano/guitar/violin.
Form rules
  • Who/What + auxiliary + subject + verb + preposition? — "Who is she talking to?" / "What are you looking at?"
  • What + be + subject + like? — "What is the weather like?" / "What was the food like?"
  • Which + noun + auxiliary + subject + ...? — "Which restaurant is he going to?"
  • Present simple question: do/does + subject + base verb — "Which team does he play for?"

Unit 47 — What...? Which...? How...? (questions 4)

What it means

What = open-ended (infinite options). Which = limited choice (from a known set). How + adjective = measurements and descriptions.

WordUseExample
Whatopen-ended, unlimited optionsWhat is your name? / What time is it? / What day is it?
Whichlimited choice, known setWhich do you prefer, tea or coffee? / Which way is the station?
Whopeople (preferred over Which)Who is older, Liz or Steve?
How + adjectivemeasurementHow old are you? / How far is it? / How much is it?
When to use What vs Which
TestUseExample
Infinite options (no list)WhatWhat is your name? / What day is it?
Limited set (choosing between known options)WhichWhich do you prefer, tea or coffee?
People (even from a limited set)Who (preferred)Who is older, Liz or Steve?

Key: "What day is it?" (open — any day of the week, no list presented). "Which day do you prefer, Monday or Tuesday?" (limited — choosing between two).

Why "What day" not "Which day"

"What day is it today?" — the answer could be any day of the week. There is no limited set being presented to choose from. "Which" requires a context where you are selecting from a known, limited set. "Which day do you prefer, Saturday or Sunday?" — now you're choosing between two specific days, so "Which" is appropriate.

Pitfalls
  • ❌ "Which day is it today?" → ✅ "What day is it today?" — open-ended (no limited set presented) → What.
  • ❌ "Which drink do you prefer?" → ✅ "Which do you prefer?" — extra word "drink" not needed; context makes it clear.
  • ⚠️ "Which is older, Liz or Steve?" → Who is preferred for people (Which is also acceptable but less natural).

How + adjective combinations

How + adjectiveMeaningExample

How fardistanceHow far is it to the station?
How longdurationHow long does it take?
How oftenfrequencyHow often do you go to the gym?

Form rules
  • What + noun — "What day is it?" / "What time is it?" / "What is your name?"
  • Which (+ noun) — "Which do you prefer?" / "Which book did you read?"
  • Who — "Who is that?" / "Who is older, Liz or Steve?"
  • How + adjective — "How old are you?" / "How far is it?" / "How much is it?"

Unit 48 — How long does it take...?

What it means

Asking and talking about duration — how much time something needs.

PatternUseExample
How long does it take to...?asking duration (general)How long does it take to fly to New York?
How long did it take [person] to...?asking duration (specific person)How long did it take him to paint the room?
It takes [time] to...stating duration (general)It takes two hours to fly to New York.
It took [person] [time] to...stating duration (specific person)It took her three hours to drive home.
When to use each pattern
  • General duration (no specific person): "How long does it take to get to the airport?" / "It takes about 30 minutes."
  • Specific person's duration: "How long did it take you to get home?" / "It took me an hour."
  • Past duration: "It took [person] [time] to [verb]." — "It took Tom two hours to finish."
  • Present duration: "It takes [person] [time] to [verb]." — "It takes me 20 minutes to walk to work."
Why "take" uses object pronouns

After "take", the person is in the object position — they are receiving the duration, not performing it. So we use object pronouns:

Subject pronounObject pronounExample with "take"
ImeIt took me an hour.
hehimIt took him two hours.
sheherIt took her all day.
weusIt took us 20 minutes.
theythemIt took them a long time.

❌ "It took they to repair the car" → ✅ "It took them to repair the car."

Pitfalls
  • ❌ "walk to home" → ✅ "walk home" — "home" is an adverb, not a noun. No preposition "to" needed. Same for: go home, come home, get home, arrive home.
  • ❌ "look for a job" → ✅ "find a job" — "look for" = the process of searching; "find" = the result. When the job was obtained, use "find".
  • ❌ "It took they to repair the car" → ✅ "It took them to repair the car" — object pronoun after "take".
  • ❌ "mintues" → ✅ "minutes" — spelling.
  • ⚠️ "How long did it take Tom to...?" — grammatically correct, but the answer key prefers the pronoun: "How long did it take him to...?"
Correct examples
  • ✅ "How long does it take to fly to New York?" — general question
  • ✅ "It takes about eight hours." — general statement
  • ✅ "How long did it take them to repair the car?" — specific person (object pronoun)
  • ✅ "It took us 20 minutes to walk home." — "home" = adverb (no "to")
  • ✅ "It took Lisa a long time to find a job." — "find" = result achieved
Form rules
  • Question: How long + do/does/did + it + take + (object pronoun) + to + verb? — "How long did it take them to repair the car?"
  • Statement (general): It + takes/took + (time) + to + verb. — "It takes two hours to fly there."
  • Statement (person): It + takes/took + (object pronoun) + (time) + to + verb. — "It took me an hour to walk home."
  • "home" = adverb: no preposition — go home, walk home, come home, get home.
  • look for = searching (process). find = result (completed). Use "find" when the outcome was achieved.
