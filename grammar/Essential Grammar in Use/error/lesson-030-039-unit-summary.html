Unit 30 — can and could

What it means

can and could are modal verbs that express ability, permission, and possibility.

VerbUseExample
canpresent ability — "I am able to do this now"I can swim. / I can't drive.
couldpast ability — "I was able to do this in the past"I could swim when I was 5.
can / couldpolite request (could is more polite)Can you help me? / Could you help me?
cangeneral possibility (it's possible in general)Smoking can damage your health.
coulduncertain possibility (maybe, not sure)It could rain tomorrow.
When to use can vs could
  • can → ability that is true right now (present): "She can speak three languages."
  • could → ability in the past: "She could read when she was three."
  • could you → polite request (present, not past): "Could you pass the salt?"
  • can't → cannot do it now: "I can't swim."
  • couldn't → could not do it in the past: "I couldn't swim when I was 5."
Why "could you" is not past

In requests, "could" is a polite form, not a past form. "Could you help me?" asks about now, not the past. It's softer/more polite than "Can you help me?" because it sounds less direct.

borrow vs lend — the #1 confusion

VerbMeaningWho is the subject?Structure
borrowtake (temporarily)the takerI borrow [something] (from you)
lendgive (temporarily)the giverYou lend [something] to me
Correct examples
  • ✅ "Could I borrow your newspaper?" (I = taker, borrow = take)
  • ✅ "Could you lend me the newspaper?" (you = giver, lend = give)
Pitfall

❌ "Could you borrow the newspaper to me?" — wrong because "borrow" means "take", not "give". You can't "take something to someone." Use "I borrow" (taker) or "you lend" (giver).

Form rules
  • can/could + bare infinitive (no "to", no -ing, no -s): can swim / could go / can't drive
  • Third person: he/she/it can (no -s!) — NOT "he cans"
  • Negative: can't (cannot) / couldn't (could not)

Unit 31 — must, mustn't, don't need to

What it means

These express necessity and prohibition.

ModalMeaningWhenExample
mustit is necessary / importantnow or futureI must go now. (= I need to go)
had toit was necessarypast (must has no past form)I had to work last Saturday.
mustn'timportant NOT to do (prohibition)don't do it — it's badYou mustn't lose these papers.
don't need tonot necessary (optional)you can if you want, but no needYou don't need to wait.
When to use must vs had to
Time markerUseExample
now / tomorrow / latermustI must phone him later today.
yesterday / last week / agohad toI had to work last Saturday.

Key: "must" has no past form. For past necessity, always use "had to".

Why mustn't ≠ don't need to

These look similar but have opposite meanings:

ModalIf you do it...Meaning
mustn't do itit's bad (dangerous/wrong)prohibition — don't do it!
don't need to do itit's fine either wayoptional — you can if you want
  • "You mustn't lose these papers." = losing is bad (prohibition)
  • "You don't need to wait for me." = waiting is optional (not necessary)
Pitfall — future vs past

❌ "I forgot to phone David yesterday. I had to phone him later today." — wrong! "later today" = future → use must.

❌ "I was very busy last week. I must work every evening." — wrong! "last week" = past → use had to.

Form rules
  • must + bare infinitive: must go / must phone / mustn't lose
  • Third person: he/she must (no -s)
  • Question: Must you...? (rare — usually "Do you have to...?")

Unit 32 — should

What it means

should gives advice or opinions — "it's a good thing to do."

PatternUseExample
should + verbpositive adviceYou should go to bed.
shouldn't + verbadvice againstYou shouldn't eat so much.
I think ... shouldgiving opinionI think you should sell it.
I don't think ... shouldadvice against (opinion)I don't think you should go to work.
Do you think I should...?asking for adviceDo you think I should learn to drive?
When to use should
  • When you want to say "it's a good idea" → should
  • When you want to say "it's not a good idea" → shouldn't
  • When asking "is it a good idea?" → Do you think I should...?
Why "not" goes after "think" (not after "should")

In the "I think" pattern, "not" attaches to the verb "think", not to "should":

  • ✅ I don't think you should go. (standard word order)
  • ❌ I think you shouldn't go. (less natural — sounds more like a direct command than an opinion)

The reason: "I don't think" frames the entire opinion as negative, which is softer/more polite than "you shouldn't" (which sounds like a direct command).

Pitfall — pronoun matching

❌ "I think he should have a holiday." (about Karen) — wrong! Karen is female → she.

Always check who the sentence is about before choosing he/she.

Form rules
  • should + bare infinitive: should go / should eat / shouldn't drive
  • Third person: he/she should (no -s)
  • No "to": should go (not "should to go")

Unit 33 — have to

What it means

have to expresses external necessity — things you must do because of rules, laws, or other people's requirements.

TenseFormExample
presenthave to / has toI have to go. / She has to work.
pasthad toI had to work last Saturday.
negativedon't have toYou don't have to go. (= not necessary)
questiondo you have to?What time do you have to get up?
When to use have to vs must
Testmusthave to
Is it your own decision/feeling?✅—
Is it an external rule/law/appointment?—✅
Is the time past?— (no past form)✅ (had to)
  • "I really must stop eating chocolate." = personal feeling
  • "You have to pay for parking." = external rule (law)
  • "I had to borrow money." = past (must has no past)
  • "You must see that film!" = strong recommendation (both work)
Why subject shifts to "you" in questions

When you ask someone about their statement, the subject converts to "you":

Their statementYour questionShift
"I have to get up early.""What time do you have to get up?"I → you
"We had to pay a lot.""How much did you have to pay?"we → you
"George had to wait.""How long did he have to wait?"George → he (stays 3rd person)

Rule: 3rd person (George, Lisa) → he/she. 1st person (I, we) → you (because you're asking the speaker).

Pitfall — have to + -ing

❌ "She doesn't have to waiting." — wrong!

✅ "She doesn't have to wait." — correct!

have to is followed by the bare infinitive (wait, work, go) — never -ing, never "to", never -s. Same rule as all modal verbs.

Unit 34 — Would you like...? I'd like...

What it means

The core distinction: like = general preference (always); would like = want now (offer/request).

QuestionMeaningWhenExample
Do you like X?Do you enjoy X in general?always"Do you like bananas?" = do you enjoy them?
Would you like X?Do you want X now?offer/invitation"Would you like a banana?" = want one now?
I like X.I enjoy X in general.always"I like coffee." = I enjoy it
I'd like X.I want X now (polite).request"I'd like a coffee." = I want one now
When to use would like vs like — the "now vs general" test
  • If the answer is about a specific moment (accepting/declining right now) → would like
  • If the answer is about a general preference (love them / all kinds / enjoy it) → do you like
Answer patternQuestion was
"Yes, I love them." (general)Do you like bananas?
"Yes, please." (specific)Would you like a banana?
"No, thank you." (specific)Would you like an ice cream?
"All kinds." (general)What kind of music do you like?
Why "would you like" is more polite than "do you want"

"Would you like" is the polite form of "do you want". Saying "Do you want a coffee?" sounds too direct/casual. "Would you like a coffee?" softens the question, making it more polite — especially with strangers or in formal settings.

Pitfall — preposition with events

❌ "Would you like to come for a concert?" — wrong!

✅ "Would you like to come to a concert?" — correct!

Events take "to": come to a concert / party / meeting / wedding. "For" works with meals ("come for dinner") but not with event nouns.

Unit 35 — Do this! Don't do that! Let's do this!

What it means

Imperatives — commands, instructions, and suggestions.

FormTypeMeaningExample
Verb!positive imperativecommand/instructionSmile! / Buy it! / Have a nice time!
Don't + verb!negative imperativeprohibition — don't do itDon't buy it! / Don't sit on the cat!
Let's + verbsuggestion"I suggest we do this together"Let's take a taxi. / Let's watch TV.
When to use imperative vs Let's
  • Telling someone else to do it → imperative (Smile! / Don't sit!)
  • Suggesting we do it together → Let's (Let's go!)

A photographer says "Smile!" (command to the subject) — not "Let's smile" (which would mean "let's both smile together").

Declining suggestions

Original question about...Negative responseExample
you (the other person)No, don't [verb]"Shall I wait?" → "No, don't wait."
we (both of us)No, let's not [verb]"Shall we go by bus?" → "No, let's not go by bus."
Pitfall — capitalization at sentence starts

❌ "OK. sleep well." — lowercase at start of new sentence.

✅ "OK. Sleep well." — capitalize after a period.

Same for: "Be careful with that vase. Don't drop it!" — "Don't" starts a new sentence.

Form rules
  • Positive imperative = bare verb (no subject): Smile! / Go! / Have!
  • Negative = Don't + bare verb: Don't go! / Don't sit!
  • Suggestion = Let's + bare verb: Let's go! / Let's take!

Unit 36 — I used to...

What it means

used to + infinitive describes past habits or states that are no longer true. "I used to play tennis" = I played tennis in the past but I don't anymore.

FormUseExample
used to + verbpast habit/state, no longer trueI used to live in London. (but not now)
didn't use to / never used topast negativeI never used to eat meat. (but I do now)
Did you use to...?past question (no -d after "did")Did you use to play tennis?
When to use used to vs present simple — the "still true?" test
Is it still true?TenseExample
Yes (still happens)present simpleShe watches TV every evening.
No (stopped)used toShe used to play volleyball.

Clue words: "when I was a child" → used to (past); "every evening" → present simple (now).

Why "much" doesn't work in positive statements
PolarityWordExample
positivea lotShe goes out a lot. ✅
negativemuchShe doesn't go out much. ✅
questionmuchDoes she go out much? ✅
positivemuchShe goes out much. ❌

Never use "much" in positive statements — use a lot or most evenings.

Pitfalls
  • ❌ "play guitar" → ✅ "play the guitar" (instruments always take "the")
  • ❌ "Did you used to play?" → ✅ "Did you use to play?" (no -d after "did" — the "d" moves to "did")
  • ❌ "I never eat meat, but I eat it now" → ✅ "I never used to eat meat, but I eat it now" (past habit needs "used to")
Form rules
  • used to + bare infinitive: used to play / used to live / used to be
  • Negative: didn't use to (no -d) / never used to
  • Question: Did you use to...? (no -d on "use" after "did")

Unit 37 — there is, there are

What it means

there is/are says that something exists or is present.

Subject typeFormExample
singular (a castle, a hospital)there is / there'sThere's a hospital.
plural (two cinemas, hotels)there are / there aren'tThere are two cinemas.
uncountable (traffic, money, water)there is / there isn't (singular!)There isn't much traffic.
question (singular)is there?Is there a bank near here?
question (plural)are there?Are there any problems?
When to use there is vs there are
  • Singular subject → there is (there's a hospital)
  • Plural subject → there are (there are two cinemas)
  • Uncountable → there is (there is much traffic / there isn't much water)

Common uncountable nouns: traffic, money, water, time, information, music, rain, snow.

Why uncountable nouns take singular

Uncountable nouns (traffic, money, water) don't have a plural form — you can't count them as individual items. In English, uncountable nouns are treated as singular, so they take "is/isn't" (never are/aren't):

  • ✅ "There isn't much traffic." (traffic = uncountable → singular)
  • ❌ "There aren't much traffic." (wrong — traffic isn't plural)

there's vs it's — the "new vs known" test

QuestionUseWhy
Am I introducing something new?there's"There's a programme I want to see." (new info)
Am I identifying something already mentioned?it's"It's a school." (answering "what's that building?")
Pitfall — missing verb

❌ "There fifty states" — no verb!

✅ "There are fifty states" — "there are" needs the verb "are".

Unit 38 — there was/were, has/have been, will be

What it means

Extending "there is/are" to past, present perfect, and future.

TenseSingularPluralWhen
pastthere wasthere werespecific past event ("when we arrived")
present perfectthere has beenthere have beenresult visible now ("police at bank")
futurethere will bethere will befuture ("when you arrive tomorrow")
When to use which tense — match to the time marker
Time markerTenseExample
"when we arrived" (past)there was/wereThere was a long queue outside.
"why are the police here?" (result now)there has/have beenThere's been a robbery.
"Now" (present fact)there areNow there are more than a thousand.
"when you arrive tomorrow" (future)there will beThere will be somebody to meet you.
"a lot of changes" (accumulated up to now)there have beenThere have been a lot of changes.
Why present perfect, not past, for "changes"

"There have been a lot of changes" — changes accumulated over time and the result is visible now ("It's very different now"). Present perfect captures this past→present bridge. Past simple ("there were") would imply all changes happened at one finished time.

Test: is there a "bridge" sentence connecting past to present? → present perfect.

Why "Now" signals present simple, not present perfect

"Now there are more than a thousand" — "Now" = present fact (a current state), not accumulated events. Use there are (present simple) for current facts, there have been for accumulated events/processes.

PhraseMeaningTense
"Now there are..."current number (fact)present simple: there are
"There have been changes"events accumulated over timepresent perfect: there have been
Pitfall — contrast clue for negative

❌ "Twenty years ago there were many tourists. Now there are a lot." — no contrast!

✅ "Twenty years ago there weren't many tourists. Now there are a lot." — contrast makes sense.

Rule: when the second sentence says "Now there are a lot", the first is likely negative (there weren't many before).

Unit 39 — It...

What it means

The pronoun it is used for weather, time, distance, and general situations.

UseExample
weather qualityIt's cold. / It's raining. / It was sunny.
time / dayIt's late. / What day is it? / It's Lisa's birthday.
distanceIt's ten kilometres. / How far is it?
it's + adjective + to + infinitiveIt's nice to see you. / It's dangerous to go out alone.
When to use it vs there for weather
TypeUseExample
weather quality (what's it like?)itIt's cold. / It's raining. / It was sunny.
weather amount (how much?)thereThere isn't much rain. / There was a lot of snow.
weather event (storm, wind)thereThere was a storm. / There was a strong wind.
Why "there" for rain amount, not "it"

"It" for weather describes what the weather is like (it's raining = the weather action is raining). But "how much rain" is an existence/amount question — does rain exist in large quantity? → "there":

  • ✅ "There isn't much rain." (amount → existence → there)
  • ❌ "It isn't much rain." (it = quality, but "isn't much rain" is about amount, not quality)

Test: Is the sentence about weather quality (what kind?) → it. Or about amount (how much?) → there.

When to use "it's + adjective + to + infinitive"

This pattern describes an opinion about an action:

  • "It's nice to see you again." (opinion: nice; action: to see you)
  • "It's dangerous to go out alone." (opinion: dangerous; action: to go out)
  • "It's impossible to work here." (opinion: impossible; action: to work)

Structure: It's + [adjective] + to + [infinitive]
