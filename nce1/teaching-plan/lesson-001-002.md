# Teaching script: Lessons 1–2

**Unit goal:** politely check ownership of a nearby object, understand an affirmative response, ask for repetition, and thank the person. At the end, the learner should do this with different familiar objects and in both roles.

**Sources:** the Lesson 1 dialogue, Lesson 2 picture drill, and their accompanying notes in the supplied photographs; see [source map](source-map.md). Method: the nine presentation steps and paired-lesson structure in [the preface](../preface.html). The changed situations and scoring tasks below are additions for our lessons.

**Materials:** source pictures; a handbag or bag, book, pen, pencil, and coat or their pictures; clear ownership cards; access to course audio or a competent reader for listening. Start with four familiar objects, then expand through the full Lesson 2 picture set during practice and review. The written workbook includes extra patterns; do not let its order replace the oral sequence.

This is a teacher's script. Reveal a model when teaching it, but keep the exit-task answers out of view until the learner responds. In text-only chat, call the exercise reading/written interaction and leave listening and pronunciation unassessed.

## Meeting A · Lesson 1 · 60 minutes

### 0–5 minutes: establish the starting point

Show an object and greet the learner. Give one easy model if needed. Check whether `Excuse me`, `your`, and `Thank you` are familiar without requiring an explanation of grammar.

If the learner already performs the exchange independently with a changed object, compress the presentation and use the time for listening discrimination and role changes. If not, teach it in the following sequence.

### 5–25 minutes: the preface's nine steps

| Step | Teacher action | Learner action / evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Introduce the story, 1 min | “A man is checking who owns a handbag.” Use Chinese briefly if necessary: 一个人想确认手提包是不是这位女士的。 | Understand the situation without translating every word. |
| 2. Understand the picture, 2 min | Show only the picture sequence, with dialogue and translation covered. Point to the two people and the bag. | Identify what is happening by pointing or a brief explanation. |
| 3. Set the listening objective, 1 min | “Listen. Is it her handbag?” Explain the task in Chinese if `her` is not yet understood. It is a comprehension prompt, not a required new production pattern. | Know what to listen for. |
| 4. First listening, 1 min | Play or read the entire source dialogue once, without stopping or displaying the transcript. | Listen for the answer; no note-taking required. |
| 5. Answer, 1 min | Ask the listening question again. Accept a point, a yes/no choice, or a brief Chinese response at this stage. | Show comprehension rather than guess from written words. |
| 6. Check meaning line by line, 4 min | Read a line, refer to a picture or act it out. Explain an unclear phrase briefly. Although the preface calls this “intensive reading”, its procedure centres on checking meaning through the pictures. | Match each line to what it does in the exchange. |
| 7. Listen again, 1 min | Replay/read the whole conversation naturally. | Follow the complete exchange now that meaning is clear. |
| 8. Repeat, 5 min | Model one short phrase at a time, pause, and let the learner respond. Repeat a difficult phrase with useful chunks rather than isolated syllables only. | Say the phrases intelligibly; practise the two different uses of `Yes`. |
| 9. Read aloud, 4 min | Reveal the transcript. Take one role, then swap. | Connect familiar spoken language to spelling and punctuation. |

The first listening is not a vocabulary examination. Do not preteach every word or reveal the answer before it. Do not insist that a beginner answer the source's `Whose ...?` question with a pattern that is formally taught later.

### Meaning and pronunciation to teach briefly

| Expression | What it does | Teaching cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Excuse me!` | Gets someone's attention politely. | Turn toward the person before asking about the object. |
| `Yes?` | Shows that the person is listening: “What is it?” | Model an inviting response, not the same delivery as a firm confirmation. |
| `Is this your handbag?` | Checks whether a nearby object belongs to the listener. | Point to the object, then the listener. Stress `handbag`, or `your` when ownership is the contrast. |
| `Pardon?` | Asks the speaker to repeat. | Use it when the message was missed; then listen again. |
| `Yes, it is.` | Confirms ownership. | End the short answer with `is`; avoid `Yes, it's` as a standalone answer. |
| `Thank you very much.` | Expresses appreciation. | Use it after the helpful response. |

Practise voiced `/ð/` in `this` and unvoiced `/θ/` in `thank`: place the tongue lightly at the teeth and feel whether the voice is on. Aim for an understandable distinction; do not spend the whole lesson perfecting one sound. Model `Is this / your handbag?` in two meaningful chunks, then join them. Avoid pronouncing every word with equal stress.

Use one short explanation of the pattern after the learner has heard and used it:

> `This is your book.` tells someone something. `Is this your book?` checks it. Move `is` before `this`. In `Yes, it is`, `it` refers to the object.

中文提示：`your` 是“你的”，后面接物品；回答里的 `it` 指刚才的物品。先会在场景中使用，再归纳语序。

### 25–35 minutes: answer, then ask

Use ownership cards so every affirmative answer is true in the exercise.

1. I ask about a book assigned to you. You answer.
2. I ask about a pen assigned to you. You answer without seeing the model.
3. You ask me about a pencil assigned to me. I answer.
4. You ask me about a bag assigned to me. I deliberately signal that I did not hear; you repeat the question.
5. Swap roles again. This time you use `Pardon?` when needed.

Tell the learner whose object each card represents before starting. Do not expect a negative response in this unit unless it is already securely known; Lessons 3–4 teach it explicitly. If a genuine negative response is needed, supply it as help rather than count it as an error.

### 35–45 minutes: cue → response → check

Demonstrate once: show a book owned by the teacher; learner asks `Is this your book?`; teacher answers. Then show four objects in random order. The learner must respond before the teacher gives any model.

After each successful question, change one element: a different object, a different speaker, or a need for repetition. If the learner says `This is your pen?`, acknowledge the meaning, model the unit's target `Is this your pen?`, and give another cue. Statement questions can occur in natural conversation; here we are deliberately practising the inverted question pattern.

If the learner omits `your`, point to the owner rather than give a long explanation. If they say only `Yes`, prompt the complete practice response. A bare `yes` is not universally rude; the fuller response helps us practise the structure and sound engaged.

### 45–55 minutes: practical task

**Situation:** a person has left a book on a shared desk. You want to check ownership.

- First round: I model the exchange, and you supply the confirmation.
- Second round: you initiate the exchange with a different object. I request repetition once.
- Third round: swap roles and remove the visible phrase prompts.

The task succeeds when the listener understands which object is being checked, ownership is confirmed, and the exchange ends appropriately. Memorising the original handbag scene alone is insufficient evidence; use a new familiar object.

### 55–60 minutes: exit and homework

Show two new object arrangements and ask the learner to initiate both exchanges. Note independent responses and any support needed. Give feedback on one useful language target and one pronunciation target only if spoken evidence is available.

**Homework, 10–12 minutes:** listen to the dialogue twice with a purpose; repeat the phrases; reconstruct it from pictures; read it once; write three ownership questions using familiar objects. If audio is unavailable, complete the oral self-practice and writing portions, and record listening as pending.

## Meeting B · Lesson 2 · 60 minutes

### 0–5 minutes: retrieve

With no transcript, return a familiar object through an exchange. Ask the learner to take the first turn. If recall fails, show one phrase cue, try again, and remove the cue on a fresh object.

### 5–10 minutes: listen for the object

Read five ownership questions in a random order with text hidden. The learner points to or selects the named picture. Use only already taught objects. Repeat once if needed. Score the intended object, not the spelling of its name.

### 10–25 minutes: extend the picture drill

Use the Lesson 2 pictured objects, a few at a time. First supply a clear spoken model for any unfamiliar noun. Then run the three-part sequence: picture/cue → learner response → model or feedback. Alternate who asks and who answers.

Include a three-minute number micro-drill using the numbers actually printed beside the pictures. Say a number; the learner finds the picture. Then the learner says a number for the teacher to find. Number recognition is a separate target from ownership-question accuracy.

### 25–40 minutes: information gap

**Task: the collection desk.** Put four familiar objects on the desk. On the first round, all belong to the teacher; the learner checks each one. On the next round, change the role so all belong to the learner. Keep labels hidden from the questioner once the task is understood. All items are deliberately owned by the respondent because the required answer at this stage is affirmative.

The teacher withholds the response until the question is clear. Include one repetition request and vary object order. The learner should not be able to complete the task by reciting the original dialogue in a fixed order. Lessons 3–4 will make the information gap richer by mixing correct and incorrect ownership claims.

### 40–50 minutes: read, then write

Read a new short exchange using a book instead of a handbag. Ask which object is being checked and why a line is repeated. Next, write a four-line exchange with a pen. Check capital letters, spaces, `your`, and the question mark.

Do selected questions from [Lesson 1](../lesson-001.html) and [Lesson 2](../lesson-002.html): ownership dialogues and question formation. Defer the Lesson 1 workbook's `What's your name?` set to Unit 04 if it is not yet known. A workbook can contain supplementary patterns beyond the main dialogue's sequence.

### 50–60 minutes: fresh check and feedback

Give five spoken object questions, five picture-based learner questions, and one unscripted ownership exchange. Do not display model answers first. Compare with the main plan's criteria; this short exit samples skills and is not a substitute for every later mastery check.

Record:

```text
Can identify the spoken object: __/5 or not assessed
Can form a fresh ownership question: __/5
Can initiate the exchange: independent / prompted
Can request repetition appropriately: yes / prompted / not tested
Can write an ownership question: accurate / needs repair
Sound target heard in actual speech:
Next review: tomorrow, 3 days, 7 days
```

## Teacher reference: models and decision points

**Source dialogue, from the supplied Lesson 1 photograph:**

```text
A: Excuse me!
B: Yes?
A: Is this your handbag?
B: Pardon?
A: Is this your handbag?
B: Yes, it is.
A: Thank you very much.
```

**New practice exchange:**

```text
A: Excuse me.
B: Yes?
A: Is this your book?
B: Yes, it is.
A: Thank you very much.
```

**If it is too easy:** increase role changes, mix all familiar objects, remove pictures for some listening trials, and revisit after a delay. Move into Unit 02 after the relevant checks; do not add unrelated vocabulary merely to make the exercise harder.

**If it is too hard:** reduce the set to two objects, model one whole question, let the learner repeat it, then vary only the object. Restore the full set gradually. Count supported success as practice, and assess independence on a later fresh attempt.

**Review task after seven days:** use a different familiar object on a desk. Ask the learner to start the conversation, respond as the other person, and write a new question. Assess listening separately with an unseen spoken cue. Advance based on the evidence, with weak skills scheduled for targeted practice.
